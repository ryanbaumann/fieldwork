"You just need better options" is the lie you tell yourself when you're tired of deciding. Late on a Sunday night a few weeks ago, my partner and I were staring at a spreadsheet of trade-offs for our next few years, and I realized I was arguing for an option I didn't even want. I've been trying to make a bunch of consequential decisions lately, and the fatigue had quietly shifted my criteria toward reasons that aren't actually important to me. Maybe that's just a function of getting older, or maybe it comes with a growing family where evenings and energy have hard limits; whatever the mix, I get it. Yet watching AI compress every workflow around us, I think judgment fatigue is maxing out for everybody right now, regardless of what stage of life they're in.

When generation gets cheap, evaluation becomes the whole game. As I wrote in [When the Answer Gets Cheap, the Question Becomes the Job](/writing/ai-saves-the-hour/), our tools eat the mechanical middle of assembling options and leave us with the harder work of deciding which constraint is binding. Aristotle drew a sharp line between technical skill (*techne*), which knows how to build a road, and practical wisdom (*phronesis*), which knows why one destination is worth the trip in a finite, messy life. When an agent or a colleague can hand you five defensible plans before breakfast, having more options doesn't make you wiser; without a clear aim, it just burns through your judgment faster until you rubber-stamp whatever looks urgent or prestigious.

![Two side-by-side panels show zero-weight motives to cut on the left and core pillars to protect on the right: irreplaceable family time, low-burn optionality, and high-agency craft and vitality.](/img/writing/why-we-make-decisions-filter.svg)

In engineering, we keep an agent or a team from drifting by writing down operating principles and [agentic evals](/work/agentic-evals/) before we inspect the output; in life, I call those principles values. What do you want out of your life, and what do you want out of your job? Being intentional about those answers is more important than ever, so my partner and I wrote ours down around a few core pillars: protecting irreplaceable windows with our family before those years pass, keeping our fixed monthly burn low so we buy autonomy today rather than deferring it to a distant exit date, and staying healthy for challenging outdoor runs and rides while doing high-agency technical craft.

The real value of that list shows up when you use it to strip zero-weight motives out of the room before you score a single option. Whenever my partner and I evaluate a major commitment at home or in my career, we sort the drivers into two buckets: we keep recurring joy, craft, and coaching capable builders, and we assign zero weight to completionism ("while we're already in here, we might as well do the whole thing"), title scarcity, or acting as a human shock absorber for a broken system. We also test the actual week rather than the pitch, because loving a problem space like enterprise AI agents won't protect your health or your family calendar if the role lacks the trust and commitment authority to cut scope when a dependency slips.

I'm still figuring out how to hold that line when a week gets noisy, and I still catch myself reaching for the easy default when I'm tired. Having our values on paper just means my partner and I can spot the drift early, pause for forty-eight hours, and make sure why we're deciding matches the life we're trying to build.
