"You just need better options" is the lie you tell yourself when you're tired of deciding. Late on a Sunday night, my partner and I stared at a spreadsheet of trade-offs for our next few years, and I caught myself arguing for a path I didn't even want. I've been trying to make a bunch of hard decisions lately, and the fatigue had quietly shifted my criteria toward reasons that aren't actually important to me. Maybe that's a function of getting older, or maybe it comes with a growing family where evenings and energy have strict limits. Whatever the mix, watching AI speed up the work around us makes one thing clear: judgment fatigue is maxing out for everybody right now, at every stage of life.

AI makes generating options cheap, which makes human judgment the bottleneck. As I found when building [an AI writing agent that doesn't write slop](/writing/can-i-build-an-ai-agent-that-doesnt-write-slop/), our tools handle the grunt work of assembling drafts and options, leaving us with the harder job of deciding what actually matters. Aristotle drew a sharp line between technical skill (*techne*), which knows how to build a road, and practical wisdom (*phronesis*), which knows why one destination is worth the trip in a finite life. When an agent or a colleague can hand you five plausible plans before breakfast, having more options doesn't make you wiser. Without a clear aim, it just burns through your judgment faster until you sign off on whatever looks urgent or prestigious.

![Two side-by-side panels show zero-weight motives to cut on the left and core pillars to protect on the right: irreplaceable family time, low-burn optionality, and high-agency craft and vitality.](/img/writing/why-we-make-decisions-filter.svg)

In engineering, we keep an agent or a team from drifting by writing down operating principles and [evaluation criteria](/work/agentic-evals/) before we grade the work; in life, I call those principles values. What do you want out of your life, and what do you want out of your job? Being intentional about those answers is more important than ever, so my partner and I wrote ours down around three pillars: protecting irreplaceable years with our family while our window together is wide open, keeping our fixed monthly burn low so we buy freedom today instead of deferring it to a distant exit date, and staying healthy for long outdoor runs and rides while doing hands-on technical work.

That list earns its keep by stripping noise out of the room before you score a single option. Whenever my partner and I weigh a major commitment at home or in my career, we separate the motives that matter from the ones that just sound convincing: we keep recurring joy, craft, and mentoring capable builders, and we give zero weight to completionism ("while we're already in here, we might as well do the whole thing"), title chasing, or serving as a human buffer for a broken system. We also test the actual Tuesday rather than the pitch, because loving a domain like enterprise AI agents won't protect your health or your family calendar if you lack the trust and authority to cut scope when a dependency slips.

I still slip when a week gets noisy, and I still catch myself reaching for the easy default when I'm tired. Putting our values on paper gives my partner and me a way to spot that drift early, pause for forty-eight hours, and check that why we're deciding matches the life we're trying to build.
